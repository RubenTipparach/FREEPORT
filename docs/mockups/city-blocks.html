What a city block is

FREEPORT calls a 10 m square with one house on it a block, lays one width of street around every one of them, and puts a dwelling on every lot in the world. Beside it here are six real plans, each a 780 m square of actual ground pulled off OpenStreetMap and drawn at the same metres to the pixel: the streets where they run and every building where it stands. Nothing under a city's name on this page is invented.

1 · The block, against six real plans at one scale

    The number that settles it is coverage: the share of the ground a place occupies that carries a building. It is the one figure both halves of this page can be held to, because it is measured either way, off OSM's own footprints for a real city and off the drawn plan for FREEPORT. Measured on the six: Chicago's Loop 55.8%, Manhattan 52.9%, Portland's downtown 46.7%, Barcelona's Eixample 42.6%, and then the two that are not grids at all, Savannah 31.7% and Siena 31.6%.

    FREEPORT today, one 10 m building on a 19 m pitch, is 27.7%: under every one of them, including a medieval hill town. At 4×4 blocks with a courtyard, a 230 m radius and the three highways run in, the drawn plan measures 43.6%, which puts it between the Eixample and Portland's downtown. The owner's arithmetic lands the town inside the real range; the 10 m block never was.

    And the slider does not climb, it SAWS, which is the 2×2 building saying something about the block it has to tile. Measured at one radius: 1 lot a side is 29.2%, 2 is 32.6, 3 is 26.6, 4 is 43.6, 5 is 32.9 and 6 is 47.9. An EVEN side tiles exactly, floor(side/2)² large buildings covering the whole block; an ODD one leaves a strip a lot wide over as yard, which is 4n²/side² and is 0.44 at three and 0.64 at five against 1.00 at two, four and six. The owner's own 4×4 is the smallest block that both holds a courtyard and divides by the building standing on it, and that is arithmetic rather than a preference.

    The town radius slider is the other half, and coverage falls as a town grows because what it grows is suburb: 46.8% at a 60 m hamlet, 44.7 at a village, 43.6 at the shipped market town, 40.6 at 300 m, 40.2 at a 540 m city and 41.5 at a 900 m capital. The grain's octave count follows the size the way town::shape's does, one octave at a hamlet and five at a capital, so a small place has one lump and a big one is ragged at every scale it has.

    At the shipped radius a whole town is about 460 m across and its arms reach half as far again down each highway, against 760 m of one real city's middle on the other tabs. A FREEPORT town is a district, not a downtown. Drag the plan to pan and use the wheel to zoom on any tab; double click puts it back.

    The older measure is how much of the ground is street, and the table keeps it because it is what the textbooks publish: 1 - (block / pitch)², the pitch being the block plus the street, a distinction this codebase already makes (town::PITCH = BLOCK + STREET). Portland's famous 260 ft grid is a 200 ft block inside a 60 ft right of way: the 260 is the pitch. Read it against the measured column and the published figure is the optimistic one every time, because a real city has alleys, slip roads and a diagonal that no pitch accounts for.

    A block holds many lots

    Manhattan's is 80 by 274 m, Chicago's and Melbourne's 100 by 200, Barcelona's 113 square, Portland's 61 square. FREEPORT's is 10 m, which is one lot, so every house in the world stands on its own island with tarmac on four sides.

    Lots front the street, two tiers deep

    Subdivision codes put it plainly: a block is sized to hold two tiers of lots of approximately equal depth, back to back, with rear boundaries or an alley between. Lots are deep and narrow, 7.6 to 15 m of frontage by 30 to 38 m deep. Nobody plats a lot with no street on it.

    The middle is not building

    Cerdà drew the Eixample with two or three sides built around a garden. Developers filled all four sides and the interior too. Either way the interior is a decision, and a 4×4 block has one to make: four lots in the middle with no frontage.

    2 · The street is not one street

    FREEPORT has exactly one street and it is a good one. Its carriageway is 5.5 m, which is precisely the 5.5 m access road in a real highways standard, and two 1.5 m pavements make the 8.5 m it lays everywhere. The defect is the everywhere: a town where the lane outside a cottage is the same width as the road through the middle has no way to tell you where you are, and nothing for a highway to arrive on.

    That is measurable now rather than asserted. Press any city tab above and the figure panel counts how many distinct rights of way the kilometre actually carries, off the map's own width and lanes tags. Portland carries 6 over its kilometre, Siena 7, Manhattan and Savannah 8, Chicago 11 and Barcelona 22. FREEPORT's is one, everywhere, on every world.

    Drawn at the cross sections' own scale and not the plan's, which is what the bar says: at a town's scale an 8.5 m street is eight pixels and the comparison this section is about cannot be read at all. Haussmann cut 137 km of boulevard through Paris at about 30 m; Cerdà's Eixample streets are 20 to 30 m with 50 m for the two great diagonals.

    3 · The land is not all houses

    Every lot in a FREEPORT town carries a dwelling. There is no shop, no works, no school, no park and no square anywhere on the planet. New York's own land use survey is the comparison, and the gap is not subtle: 16.1% of New York is open space and 0.0% of FREEPORT is.

      FREEPORT's rows are counted off the drawing above, not asserted, and two of them are deliberately off New York's: commercial is high because the frame holds one town centre where the survey holds five boroughs, and street is high because the ranks in section two are wider than the 32.0% a uniform local grid costs and three arterials now run through the plan. An avenue is paid for in ground, and so is a road out of town. The palette is the planner's own: yellow to brown for residential, orange to red for commercial, purple for industry, blue for institutions, green for open space. There is no legally mandated standard for it, which is why every zoning map carries a legend.

      4 · A city grows along its roads

      The owner's own correction, and it is the thing rings cannot say. FREEPORT's demand field is 1 - dist / edge(bearing) with a fractal bite: it falls off from a town's middle in every direction at once, so a town is a bitten disc wherever its roads happen to arrive. That is exactly Burgess's concentric zone model, published in 1925 and already thought too simple by 1939.

      A real town does not spread evenly. It reaches out along every road that reaches it, because the road is what the place is for: frontage on it is worth more than ground behind it, so building follows the line and keeps following it long past where a ring would have stopped. That is ribbon development, it is Hoyt's sectors, and it is visible in every real plan on this page. The FREEPORT tabs above carry it now: three arterials, the town reaching 0.95 of its own radius further down each one, the high street strung along them a block deep, and the fringe out there frayed by the same fractal grain the rest of the edge already has.

      And the highway has to MERGE, which is a second thing. Drawn as a bar that stopped at a radius it ended in mid air with the town's grid carrying on past it, joined to nothing. A trunk road that reaches a town does not end: it becomes the high street. So it tapers, from the country's own 22 m out past the town down to the local STREET of 8.5 m by the core, and all three stop at the market square, because a road that has arrived at one has arrived. The game's own road::slip is the curved version of the same statement, a cubic Hermite from the highway's mouth to the nearest crossing the town paved.

      Three rules were tried for what an oblique road does to a block grid, and the third is the only one that is right in the picture and in the number at once. Rejecting every block within a margin of a third of a block put 14 m of bare ground either side of a 20 m road and the arterial read as a gap in the plan. Rejecting on the block's own support function, which is the honest geometric test for an overlap, sterilises a band as wide as a block's own diagonal whatever the road is: 53 m at 4×4, which cut the town into three wedges and dropped coverage from 43.6% to 42.0%. What it does instead is ask the question of a LOT: a block the road runs clean over is gone, and a block it merely clips keeps every lot the road does not stand on. One function (onRoad) is asked by the lot that is drawn and by the footprint that is counted, so a building under a road cannot be in one and not the other, and the roads are drawn LAST so a block painted after one cannot chop it into stubs.

      Burgess, 1925: rings

      Uniform rings around one centre. This is what the code does today, and it is why every town reads the same from the air whatever is around it.

      Hoyt, 1939: sectors

      Growth follows the transport corridors, so land use is wedges and not rings: industry down the rail line, the good addresses up the other side. FREEPORT already knows every bearing it needs and uses none of them: the atlas has the roads that reach each town, and settle computes the shore axis. This is the cheap one and it is what the plan above is drawn on.

      Harris and Ullman, 1945: nuclei

      A big city has several centres, not one: a satellite business district forms wherever a suburb got large enough. For FREEPORT this is the cheapest of the three, because demand is a field and a second nucleus is a second peak in it.

      5 · The square, which is where a town has a middle

      A market square is the one piece of a town that is not a building and not a road, and the civic buildings face it. Its size is a block count, which is what makes it cheap here: Place des Vosges is 140 m square, which is three of these blocks and the two streets between them (137 m). Wrocław's market square is 213 by 178 m, Siena's Campo about a hundred across.

      So a square is a block group with its internal streets taken out and its paving left in, and the blocks that face it build civic rather than residential. Nothing new is needed to draw one: it is the paving the town already lays and the building kinds it already has.

      What this suggests for FREEPORT

      Downtown: filled, 2×2 large

      Four large buildings of 2×2 lots each fill the block, every one a corner fronting two streets. The owner's own arithmetic, and a Manhattan tower block.

      Town: hollow, 12 round a court

      The twelve perimeter lots build, the 2×2 interior is a courtyard. Every building fronts a street. Cerdà's hollow block.

      Suburb: hollow and sparse

      The same perimeter, thinned by the fractal grain the towns already carry, interior as gardens. What makes a suburb a suburb is the space, not the house.

      Three street ranks, not one

      Local 8.5 m as now, a 12.5 m collector every fourth line, a 20 m avenue on the two trunks home_run already paves. The highway's slip lands on an avenue, which is what an arterial is for.

      A use, not just a height

      A block gets a use as well as a storey count: commercial on the avenue, civic on the square, industry in a wedge by the road in, open space to about a sixth of the town.

      Arms down the roads

      One term in demand: for every road that reaches a town, a bearing it reaches further down. The plan above runs 0.95 of its own radius out along each of three, which is what turns a bitten disc into a place with somewhere to arrive from.

      Sectors, then a second centre

      Hoyt before Harris and Ullman: make the use a function of bearing as well as distance first, since both bearings already exist, and leave a second nucleus for the day a town is big enough to want one.

      What it would cost, named rather than hidden

      Large buildings need a re-bake

      The library is thirteen Blender bakes at width 10.0, depth 10.0. A 2×2 building is 20 m, so the large kinds want a bake at the new footprint. The guard exists already: Bake::models refuses a bake that does not fit the block its kind covers.

      New kinds for new uses

      A shop, a works, a school and a bandstand are building kinds this world does not have. model::Kind is a match arm and the parametric fallback builds any of them, but each wants a bake to look as good as the five that exist.

      An arm has to be LEVELLED

      This is the one that is not free. Site::level_r is edge(bearing) + APRON, so the ground a town flattens follows its outline: an arm that adds demand puts blocks outside that plateau, which is the buried suburb this repository already measured at 1.13 m once. So town::edge has to gain the same term, which means a Site has to carry the bearings of the roads that reach it, which means the sites are installed after the network is routed. The atlas has the roads; the order is what moves.

      A block a highway CLIPS is a partial block

      The plan above keeps every lot an arterial does not stand on, which the canvas can do because a lot is a rectangle it either fills or skips. In the game a lot carries a baked Model, so the same rule is town::plot refusing a lot whose own footprint meets the road's right of way, and the road is road::corridor rather than a ray. It costs a corridor query per lot at plan time, which is what Planet::around already filters for a chunk.

      Every street number moves

      PITCH is read by the street mesh, the traffic graph, the route a stolen car takes out of town and the slip a highway joins on. A second and third street width is the first time any of them has had to ask which street.
